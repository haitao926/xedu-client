# Advanced Course Design Framework

Use this framework to design or repair a course before choosing XEdu experiment forms. It combines established methods into one decision sequence rather than treating any single model as a checklist.

| Method | Design contribution | Primary or institutional source |
| --- | --- | --- |

## Integrated Design Method

### 1. Frame

Define the standards or competency source, unit boundaries, authentic use context, time, constraints, and non-negotiable outcomes. Distinguish external curriculum authority from local project notes.

### 2. Model the learner

Build a hypothesis about prior knowledge, component skills, misconceptions, motivation, variability, and access. Identify what evidence confirms or disproves the hypothesis. Do not turn assumptions into facts.

### 3. Specify desired results

Separate four levels:

- `transfer_goals`: what learners can independently use in a new situation;
- `enduring_understandings`: explanatory ideas and relationships worth retaining;
- `essential_questions`: recurring questions that organize inquiry;
- `knowledge_and_skills`: facts, concepts, procedures, and component abilities.

Convert them into observable objectives with action, conditions, and performance level.

### 4. Design the evidence system

Use a claim-evidence-task structure:

- `claim`: what competence or understanding is asserted;
- `evidence`: what observable behavior/product would support the claim;
- `task`: what situation will elicit that evidence;
- `criteria`: what distinguishes emerging, sufficient, and strong performance.

Balance diagnostic, formative, and summative evidence. A test, quiz, screenshot, or artifact is defined by how its evidence is used, not by its format.

### 5. Build the learning progression

Map prerequisite and component skills before sequencing lessons. Move from supported performance to independent transfer using only the scaffolds the learner model justifies. Useful patterns include:

- activate/retrieve prior knowledge;
- model or analyze a worked example;
- guided practice with feedback;
- independent practice;
- compare concrete and abstract representations;
- apply in a varied or unfamiliar context;
- explain, retrieve, and reflect after a delay.

Avoid both premature discovery for novices and permanent over-scaffolding. Fade support as evidence of competence appears.

### 6. Plan formative decisions

For every checkpoint specify:

1. What evidence will be elicited from all learners?
2. What response patterns are expected?
3. What do those patterns imply about learning?
4. What will the teacher and learners do next?

Feedback must be actionable and followed by an opportunity to use it.

### 7. Design for variability

Use UDL to reduce irrelevant barriers while preserving the same learning goal and criteria. Offer purposeful options for engagement, representation, and expression. Add accessibility, language, pacing, equipment, network, and collaboration contingencies.

### 8. Audit coherence and load

Check vertical coherence across lessons and horizontal alignment within each lesson. Remove activities that do not advance an objective or elicit evidence. Check transitions, tool switching, working-memory demands, time, safety, and the difference between productive challenge and incidental difficulty.

### 9. Select resources and forms

Choose resources only after the learning function is clear. Every tool must either represent content, enable practice, elicit evidence, provide feedback, or support transfer. Existing assets may be reused, changed, reordered, archived, or rejected.

Use this repair decision rule:

| Evidence | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Advances an objective, has a distinct learning function, and fits the progression | `retain` |
| Useful content or interaction, but its role, timing, scaffold, or assessment use is misaligned | `repurpose_or_reorder` |
| Duplicates another resource, adds incidental load, or has no objective/evidence link | `archive` |
| Alignment cannot be judged from available information | `defer_and_collect_evidence` |

A legacy design requires repair when any transfer goal is unassessed, an objective lacks evidence or criteria, an activity lacks a learning function, an assessment cannot inform a claim, the sequence assumes an unverified prerequisite, or the course collapses when one preferred tool is unavailable.

### 10. Evaluate and iterate

Collect implementation evidence about learner performance, timing, misconceptions, participation, tool friction, and contingency use. Revise the learner model, alignment, sequence, assessment, or resources based on evidence rather than preference.

## High-Level Quality Questions

- Do transfer goals go beyond completing the named artifact?
- Does every objective have evidence and explicit criteria?
- Does every activity have a learning function?
- Can the progression explain why this lesson comes before the next?
- Are novice support and later independence both visible?
- Does formative evidence trigger a defined response?
- Are access barriers reduced without lowering the goal?
- Would the course remain coherent if a preferred tool were unavailable?
